## Main finding

Binotto and Delgado already compare validation metrics for ordinal hyperparameter selection, with outer cross-validation and sensitivity to rightmost interval boundaries. Their interval endpoint is a numeric class boundary, not endpoint-class recall, and metric imbalance weights are not balanced-versus-unweighted training. Metric-dependent selection, nested-style evaluation and endpoint sensitivity are not individually new.

Conditional direction for Astra: shared fixed candidate predictions, crossing training class weighting with validation selection metric and evaluating held-out metric-specific selection regret, endpoint-class recall/errors and tradeoffs. This is operationally distinguishable from the inspected metric comparisons, direct training loss optimization and decoder changes; it is not proof of novelty or publishability. ## Primary papers

### Binotto and Delgado 2025

Adapting performance metrics for ordinal classification to interval scale: length matters, Machine Learning 114, 41, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10994-024-06654-4. Publisher full text sections 2–5 and conclusions inspected at https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10994-024-06654-4; table cells and figures not independently extracted. Three datasets: Facial Age 9,673 images with 2,000-instance training subsamples, Abalone 4,177 and Parkinson telemonitoring 5,875 recordings from 42 people. RF and kNN use ten outer folds and three inner tuning folds; selection metrics are error rate, standardized ordinal MAE and interval MAE. RF random search has 10 iterations and kNN searches k=1–20. They vary five upper interval boundaries. Identical realized RF candidate pools across metrics are not established. Their theoretical cost-sensitive TC weights class counts and interval densities but section 5 highlights interval MAE rather than TC; neither demonstrates a training class-weight factorial. Authors report conditional, preliminary results varying by dataset, model and interval length. Scores across lengths change definition; random folds of repeated Parkinson recordings do not establish person-independent generalization. Fixed shared-pool training-weight by selector by endpoint-class recall interaction not identified in section 5, which is not proof of absence elsewhere.

### Cruz-Ramirez et al 2014

Metrics to guide a multi-objective evolutionary algorithm for ordinal classification, Neurocomputing 135, 21–31, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neucom.2013.05.058. Publisher abstract https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0925231213011399 inspected. Cardiff manuscript https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/112884/1/2014-Metrics%20to%20guide%20a%20multi-objective%20evolutionary%20algorithm%20for%20ordinal%20classification.pdf located through OpenAlex but returned 403 verification challenge; access control respected and full text not read. Evolutionary optimization of an ordinal neural network uses metric pairs; MMAE measures worst class mean absolute error, MAE overall error. ### de la Torre et al 2018

Publisher abstract/highlights https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167865517301666 inspected, not full text. Three real problems compare deep models trained directly using weighted kappa versus log loss; authors report improved classification in their cases. Dataset specifics, training class weights, validation selectors and endpoint outcomes are unverified. This changes training rather than only reselecting identically trained candidates.

### Delgado 2026

Ordinal Classification with Label-Dependent Loss, Machine Learning 115, 60, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10994-026-07023-z. Full publisher HTML https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10994-026-07023-z sections 1 and 5 and Appendix C inspected; table cells/figures not independently extracted. Two datasets: 4,945 restricted age-image cases and 1,599 red-wine cases collapsed to three classes. Age RF uses three-fold random-search tuning by error rate, standardized MAE or cost-weighted MAE with ten-fold outer evaluation; wine compares 20 cumulative-link specifications. Same predicted probabilities are decoded using different rules, not a fixed class-weight candidate selection factorial. Asymmetric costs are decision/evaluation weights; balanced-versus-unweighted training not identified in inspected sections. Authors report aligned-cost improvement alongside more distance-2 errors in wine. Loss, score, decision and metric distinctions already appear explicitly. The paper claims its proposed score is proper; the displayed formula is linear in probabilities, so general properness requires independent verification and is not relied on here.
